# Capablanca's Principles

José Raúl Capablanca (1888–1942) was the 3rd World Chess Champion and widely regarded as one of the most naturally gifted players in history. His style was crystal-clear: no unnecessary complications, no wasted moves, no weaknesses. He won games by making them look effortless.

His principles aren't flashy — they're the bedrock of good positional chess. Beginners who internalize them skip years of bad habits.

---

## The Principles

### 1. Improve Your Least Active Piece
This is Capablanca's most famous piece of advice and arguably the most useful single rule in chess.

Before you launch an attack, before you pursue any plan — ask yourself: **"Which of my pieces is doing the least right now?"** Then improve it.

- A Knight sitting on the rim doing nothing? Reroute it to the center.
- A Bishop blocked behind your own pawns? Open a diagonal or trade it for something.
- A Rook still on its starting square? Put it on an open file.

The logic: if one of your pieces is idle, you're fighting with a smaller army. An attack with five active pieces beats an attack with three active pieces and two bystanders.

> **Ask every move:** "What is my worst piece? How can I make it better?"

---

### 2. Avoid Creating Weaknesses
Capablanca rarely created weak pawns in his own position. A weakness is any pawn or square that is hard to defend and easy for your opponent to target.

Common weaknesses to avoid:
- **Isolated pawns** — a pawn with no friendly pawns on adjacent files. It can never be defended by another pawn and becomes a permanent target.
- **Doubled pawns** — two pawns stacked on the same file. Usually one of them is weak.
- **Backward pawns** — a pawn that can't advance because the square in front is controlled by an enemy pawn. The square in front of it (often an open file) becomes a great outpost for your opponent.
- **Holes** — squares in your position that can never be defended by a pawn and that your opponent can occupy permanently.

This doesn't mean never accept a weakness — sometimes it's worth it. But you should always know what weakness you're creating and what you're getting in return.

---

### 3. Simplify When You're Ahead
When you have a material advantage, trade pieces — not pawns.

The reasoning is straightforward: with fewer pieces on the board, your extra material becomes more decisive. A Rook advantage is winning in an endgame but just an advantage in the middlegame. Trading down removes your opponent's chances for counterplay and brings you closer to the win.

- Up a piece? Trade pieces.
- Up a pawn in the endgame? Trade Rooks (usually).
- Don't trade pawns unnecessarily — you need them in the endgame.

Capablanca's endgame technique was legendary precisely because he used this principle ruthlessly: win a pawn, simplify, convert.

---

### 4. Activate Your King in the Endgame
In the middlegame, your King hides. In the endgame, your King fights.

Once Queens and most pieces are off the board, centralizing your King is often the most important thing you can do. A King in the center controls key squares, supports its own pawns, and attacks enemy pawns.

- March your King toward the center as soon as the endgame begins.
- In King-and-pawn endgames, King position is often the *only* thing that matters.
- Opposition (Kings directly facing each other) is a key weapon — see [[Endgame]].

> A King that stays on the back rank in the endgame is a liability. A King in the center is a piece.

---

### 5. The Bishop Pair Is an Advantage
Two Bishops vs. a Bishop and Knight (or two Knights) is usually an edge, especially in open or semi-open positions.

Why: Bishops cover long diagonals and can attack both sides of the board simultaneously. Two Bishops together can cover every color. In positions with open lines and pawns on both sides, the bishop pair is a long-term structural advantage.

When it matters most:
- Open positions with pawns on both wings
- Endgames with pawns spread across the board
- When your opponent's Knights have no strong outposts

When it matters less:
- Closed positions where Bishops are blocked by pawns
- Positions where Knights can occupy strong, fixed outpost squares

---

### 6. Rooks Belong on Open Files
A Rook on a closed file is doing almost nothing. Put Rooks on open files (no pawns) or semi-open files (only enemy pawns), where they exert real pressure.

- **Open file:** A file with no pawns at all — both Rooks can contest it.
- **Semi-open file:** Your pawn is gone, but an enemy pawn remains — your Rook pressures the enemy pawn.
- **7th rank:** A Rook on the 7th rank is devastating — it attacks pawns that haven't moved and can trap the enemy King on the back rank.
- **Doubled Rooks:** Two Rooks on the same open file (one behind the other) are one of the most powerful formations in chess.

---

### 7. Don't Rush — Improve Before You Attack
Capablanca almost never launched premature attacks. His approach: improve every piece to its best square first, then — once everything is optimized — the position will often *tell you* what the plan is.

Rushing an attack before your pieces are ready is usually punished. Patient preparation wins. This connects directly to Principle #1: if any of your pieces can still be improved, improving them takes priority over attacking.

---

## Capablanca's Principles as a Checklist

When you don't know what to do (no forcing moves, no clear plan), run through this:

1. Which of my pieces is least active? Improve it.
2. Am I about to create a weakness? Is it worth it?
3. Am I ahead in material? Have I simplified enough?
4. Is my King active (endgame) or safe (middlegame)?
5. Do I have the bishop pair? Am I using it?
6. Are my Rooks on open files or the 7th rank?
7. Are *all* my pieces ready before I attack?
