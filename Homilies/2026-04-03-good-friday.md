# Tremble and Stay: The Still, Dark Center of History

*"Were you there when they crucified my Lord? Sometimes it causes me to tremble, tremble, tremble..."*

The old spiritual does not ask whether you understand the crucifixion. It asks whether you were *there* — and it warns you what proximity costs. Today, of all days, we are asked to be there. Good Friday is the still, dark center of all of history: the day Christians believe that God Himself — in the person of Jesus of Nazareth — was stripped, beaten, nailed to a cross, and left to die between two criminals on a hill called Golgotha, "the place of the skull." It is the most paradoxical day in human religion: the death of the one believed to be Life itself.

*"He was wounded for our transgressions; he was crushed for our iniquities; upon him was the chastisement that brought us peace, and with his stripes we are healed"* (Isaiah 53:5). Isaiah wrote those words centuries before Rome ever raised a cross. Today we go down into them — all the way down. Do not rush. There is something dishonest about jumping from Palm Sunday straight to Easter. Good Friday demands that we sit, as the disciples sat, in the darkness, and feel the full weight of what love costs.

## The Blood on the Doorposts

Before there was Good Friday, there was Passover. You cannot understand the cross without first understanding the blood on the doorposts. The Last Supper was a Passover Seder; Jesus was crucified at Passover. This was no coincidence. The entire sacrificial architecture of the Hebrew Bible was aimed, like an arrow in flight, at Golgotha.

Maimonides saw the strange logic of that first Passover: the very act which was then considered the cause of death would become the cause of deliverance from death. *"When I see the blood, I will pass over you"* (Exodus 12:13). Death looks at the household marked with the lamb's blood and moves on. Not a bone of that lamb was to be broken (Exodus 12:46) — and the soldiers, finding Jesus already dead, did not break his legs (John 19:33-36). When John the Baptist cried, *"Behold, the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world!"* (John 1:29), he was not reaching for a metaphor. He was naming the fulfillment of a fifteen-hundred-year rehearsal.

The Hebrew word for sacrifice carries the secret. **Korban** comes from *karav* — to draw near. Sacrifice was never primarily about appeasement; it was about proximity — tearing away a part of one's inner animal egoism for the sake of drawing closer to the Creator. And the Zohar adds the word this day needs most: *"There is no light except that which comes from darkness."* The Lurianic mystics spoke of *tzimtzum* — God's self-contraction to make space for the world. Paul spoke of *kenosis*: Christ *"emptied himself"* (Philippians 2:7). On Good Friday, God once again withdraws, empties, contracts — entering the broken vessel of human mortality — so that from that shattering, repair and redemption can flow.

## The Great Reversal

Here is the scandal we must not explain away: the Creator died as a criminal. Paul, the earliest theologian of the cross, felt its offense fully: *"The word of the cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved, it is the power of God"* (1 Corinthians 1:18).

Our natural religious instinct — Luther called it the theology of glory — expects God to appear in power, success, beauty, and triumph. We look for God on the mountaintop, in the miracle, in the victory. But Luther insisted that this is precisely where God hides himself. *"God can only be found in suffering and the cross."* The cross is the great reversal: what the world calls foolish is wisdom, what the world calls weakness is power, what looks like defeat is victory, and what looks like God-forsakenness is the very moment of redemption.

And for whom was this done? Paul will not let us soften it: *"While we were yet sinners, Christ died for us... when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son"* (Romans 5:8, 10). Not for the righteous. Not for those who deserved it. For enemies. The movement of God in the cross is entirely prior to any human response — the Father running toward the prodigal while he is still a great way off. The measure of the cross is that it reaches to the furthest depths of human guilt and shame.

How does this death save? The church has never settled on a single formula, and perhaps that is fitting — Lewis observed that you do not need to know the biochemistry of food to be nourished. But each of the great pictures shows us a facet of the diamond. The cross is *victory*: Christ enters the enemy's territory — death itself — and destroys it from within, *"having disarmed the powers and authorities, he made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them by the cross"* (Colossians 2:15). The cross is *payment*: Aquinas judged Christ's passion not merely sufficient but superabundant — by suffering out of love and obedience, Christ gave more to God than was required to compensate for the offense of the whole human race, because of the exceeding charity from which He suffered, the dignity of the life He laid down, and the depth of the grief endured. The cross is *love that transforms*: the sight of God dying for enemies breaks the stony heart open. The cross is *healing*: Gregory of Nyssa saw Christ reliving the human story Adam failed, repairing human nature from the inside, God's love working like fire on gold and dross. And the cross is *solidarity*: like the scapegoat of the Day of Atonement, driven into the wilderness bearing sins it did not commit (Leviticus 16), *"God made him who had no sin to be sin for us"* (2 Corinthians 5:21).

And why a cross, specifically — why not a quiet death? Aquinas answers, following Augustine: among all kinds of death, none was more execrable, more fear-inspiring than this. Christ chose the most shameful possible death so that no kind of death would remain beyond the reach of redemption, and no depth of human humiliation would be foreign to God. The cross reaches to the bottom of the pit, so no one need fear they are beneath its reach.

Lewis gave the whole mystery its most memorable image: the great lion killed on the Stone Table in the traitor's stead. The Witch knew the Deep Magic — but there is a magic deeper still, which she did not know: when a willing victim who had committed no treachery was killed in a traitor's stead, the Table would crack and Death itself would start working backwards.

## Seven Words from the Cross

Across the four Gospels, tradition preserves seven sayings of Jesus from the cross. Listen to their arc: it begins with forgiveness for enemies and ends with complete trust in the Father. Even in its darkest moment, the cross is not despair. It is an act of perfect love and surrender.

**"Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do"** (Luke 23:34). His first word under torture is a prayer for his torturers. This is not passive resignation; it is active love under torture. The soldiers dividing his garments are not the only ones forgiven — "them" has no limit. It includes everyone who, in spiritual blindness, crucifies Love. It includes us.

**"Today you will be with me in paradise"** (Luke 23:43). The most scandalous word of mercy in all of Scripture. The thief offers no confession of doctrine, no baptism, no sacrament — just *"remember me."* And Jesus answers: *Today.* The dying thief did not understand the Trinity or the atonement; he simply trusted the person hanging beside him. Faith precedes understanding. Spurgeon, who knew this wave-tossed grace, said it best: *"I have learned to kiss the wave that throws me against the Rock of Ages."*

**"Woman, behold your son... Behold your mother"** (John 19:26-27). Pastoral care from the cross. Dying by slow asphyxiation, Jesus takes the time to ensure his mother will be cared for — and in doing so forms a new family at the foot of the cross, a kinship not of blood but of love, in which no one is left orphaned.

**"My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?"** (Matthew 27:46; Mark 15:34). The most shattering verse in Scripture: God cries out to God in desolation. But hear what Jesus is doing — he is praying Psalm 22, which begins in absolute abandonment and ends in vindication: *"All the ends of the earth will remember and turn to the Lord."* Even in agony, he is anchored in Scripture. John of the Cross would recognize this as the dark night writ cosmic: *"This dark night is an inflow of God into the soul."* The darkness is luminous; the silence is the loudest communication. And Luther gave the most radical answer of all: Christ genuinely entered Godforsakenness — became the forsaken one — so that we need never be ultimately forsaken. No human being can fall lower than where Christ was on the cross. No abandonment, no addiction, no depression, no grief, no shame is beyond the reach of the One who was there first.

Even Viktor Frankl, in Auschwitz — the closest modern analog to crucifixion — watched men share their last bread and discovered what the cross displays: *"In some way, suffering ceases to be suffering at the moment it finds a meaning, such as the meaning of a sacrifice."* If that is true for a prisoner in the camps, how much more on Calvary, where suffering finds the ultimate meaning: the redemption of the world.

**"I thirst"** (John 19:28). Two words in Greek. The one who offered living water to the woman at the well (John 4:10) now thirsts; the source of life is dying. But the mystics heard more: even in his physical thirst, Jesus thirsts for souls — that the lost be found, that the separated be reunited. God on the cross thirsts for us.

**"It is finished"** (John 19:30). In Greek, a single word: *Tetelestai* — written across certificates of debt when they were paid in full, called out by runners completing the race, spoken by artists finishing a masterpiece. It is the cry of a victor, not a victim. Not "I am dying." Not "I have failed." A declaration: it is accomplished. Paul saw the ledger: God cancelled *"the record of debt that stood against us with its legal demands... nailing it to the cross"* (Colossians 2:14). Done. Done completely. Done with surplus.

**"Father, into thy hands I commit my spirit"** (Luke 23:46). Psalm 31:5 — the Psalms are the prayer book of Jesus even on the cross. The first word from the cross was "Father"; the last word is "Father." This is the most complete act of trust ever performed: everything — existence itself — deposited into Another's hands. Eckhart taught that the soul grows by subtraction, not addition. At the seventh word the subtraction is total — and precisely there, in total self-giving, is the fullness.

## The Torn Veil and the Torn Heart

All three synoptic Gospels record that from noon until three, darkness covered the land (Matthew 27:45; Mark 15:33; Luke 23:44). Creation itself mourned. And the Temple curtain — the thick veil sealing the Holy of Holies — was torn in two from top to bottom. The barrier between God and humanity, maintained through sacrifice and priestly ritual for a thousand years, was destroyed. The way into the presence of God — formerly reserved for one priest, one day a year — is now open to all, always. Good Friday tears open the veil.

But the mystics will not let the cross remain an event outside us. It must become an event in the soul. Paul: *"I have been crucified with Christ. It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me"* (Galatians 2:20). The Christian does not merely benefit from the crucifixion; the Christian participates in it. The old self — bound to selfishness, fear, and death — is crucified. A new self rises. Good Friday is not only about what happened *to* Christ; it is about what happens *in* us.

Eckhart said, *"God is at home. It is we who have gone out for a walk"* — and the cross is the day we begin to come home, because it strips away the false self and opens the ground of the soul where God already dwells. John of the Cross reached the same place through his *nada*: on that hill, Christ has nothing — no home, no dignity, no followers, no consolation — and from that absolute nothingness, everything emerges. *"Where there is no love, put love — and you will find love."* And Teresa of Ávila stood fiercely against any spirituality that would climb past the wounded humanity of Jesus to some purer height. That humanity is the gate. *"Christ has no body now but yours. No hands, no feet on earth but yours."* The cross is not only something to contemplate. It is something to embody.

For see what this day reveals about God. God is not a safe God — this God bleeds. God's love is not conditional — it moves toward us while we are still enemies. God takes justice seriously and love seriously at once — the cross is where *"justice and mercy kiss"* (Psalm 85:10). And God enters the worst of human experience — betrayal by a friend, denial by companions, mockery, injustice, the death of dignity, the feeling of being utterly forsaken. Whatever you are suffering, he has been there. Not theologically. Actually.

And do not miss what the cross says about you. If the Son of God died for humanity — if this was the price worth paying — then every human being carries a dignity beyond calculation. The cross does not merely rescue. It dignifies.

## Stay at the Cross

Tomorrow is Holy Saturday — the longest silence. The disciples did not know it was temporary; for them, Saturday was not a waiting room for Easter but the end, darkness without dawn. There are seasons of every life that feel like that Saturday. Yet the creed insists: *he descended into hell* — present even in the lowest, most forsaken place. Nachman of Breslov, who knew darkness intimately, said the world is a very narrow bridge, and the essential thing is not to be afraid. Holy Saturday is the narrowest point of the bridge. But the bridge holds.

And then — but that is not today's story. Today we stay at the cross.

So here is the demand of this day, and every voice we have heard — Paul's scandal, Augustine's restless heart, Aquinas's superabundance, Luther's thunder, Lewis's deeper magic, the mystics' nothingness, even Frankl's witness from the camps — converges upon it: **Good Friday cannot be merely observed. It must be inhabited.**

For the intellect: sit with the scandal that the Creator died as a criminal. Do not rush past it; do not explain it away. Let its impossibility crack open your categories. For the heart: allow the love displayed on the cross to do something in you — let it soften you toward enemies, toward the suffering, toward the self you have tried so hard to protect. For the will: take up the daily crucifixion of the false self, for there is a self we must lose before we can be found. And for whatever suffering you carry today: bring it to that cross. Not to be explained. Not to be made comfortable. But to be placed in the hands that were nailed open — open enough to receive the whole weight of the world.

Good Friday is the day God paid a debt He did not owe, because we owed a debt we could not pay. *Tetelestai.* The debt is paid in full. There is nothing left for you to add — as if someone paid an astronomical debt for you and you insisted on chipping in a dollar. There is only the prayer this day requires. Not "I understand." Not "It is fair." Not even "I am not afraid." Simply: *"Into your hands."*

*Were you there when they crucified my Lord?*

Tremble. And stay.
