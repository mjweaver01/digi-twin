# The Name Spoken in the Garden: An Easter Sunday of Sacred Reading

It is early on the first day of the week, and it is still dark. A woman is walking to a tomb. She comes before she has any reason to hope — she comes looking for a corpse. And because this is where Easter actually begins, this is where we will begin too: not in the light, but in the dark; not with answers, but with weeping; not with doctrine, but with a name.

Easter is the pivot of the entire Christian story — the event upon which everything stands or falls. Paul said it most plainly: *"If Christ be not risen, then is our preaching vain, and your faith is also vain"* (1 Corinthians 15:14). Remove the resurrection and the whole structure collapses. Christianity is not a philosophy or a set of ethics — it is a claim about something that actually happened, in a body, in a tomb, on a specific Sunday morning. But the resurrection did happen, and Paul's great declaration rings out: *"But now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept... For as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive"* (1 Corinthians 15:20–22). Death is defeated, and new creation has begun.

The old monks had a way of receiving this news that our hurried age has nearly forgotten. They called it *Lectio Divina* — sacred reading. Read slowly. Meditate on the word that arrests you. Pray it back to God. Then rest in silence. Rather than reading *at* the text, you allow the text to read *you*. Origen taught that Scripture, like the human person, has body, soul, and spirit — a literal meaning, a moral meaning, a mystical meaning. So let us walk the resurrection texts that way today, from the garden to the road to the single question that waits at the end.

## The Name in the Dark

Mary Magdalene stays at the tomb when everyone else goes home. Peter and the other disciple look in, see the linen strips, and leave. Mary stays. She weeps. She asks the angels, she asks the stranger — *"Where have they taken Him?"* — and receives silence. She mistakes the Risen Lord for a gardener. Then one word: *"Mary."* And everything changes.

Notice: the first witness to the resurrection is a weeping woman who almost missed it entirely because she was looking for a corpse. Mary comes to the tomb with a *concept* of Jesus — a body to be anointed, a location to be found. The resurrection shatters her concept entirely. Gregory of Nyssa warned us: *"Concepts create idols; only wonder grasps anything."* The empty tomb is the moment all our concepts of God are emptied out — so that the living God can speak.

Teresa of Ávila called prayer *"an intimate friendship, a frequent heart-to-heart conversation with Him by whom we know ourselves to be loved"* — and friendship is built on exactly this: knowing and being known by name. Not a concept. Not a doctrine. A name. John of the Cross knew Mary's tomb as the dark night that precedes the dawn — but notice: she had to stay in the darkness to receive the dawn. You do not have to be in the light to be found by the Light. You only have to remain. The ones who receive the resurrection are not the ones who had it all together. They are the ones who stayed.

Where in your life is Jesus calling your name right now, and you haven't yet recognized Him?

## The Foundation That Cannot Shake

From the intimacy of the garden, Paul takes us to the open sky. In 1 Corinthians 15 he gives us the seed: you do not plant a rose and expect a rose to come up. You plant something small, dry, seemingly dead — and what emerges is transfigured. Same life, utterly different form. *"It is sown in corruption; it is raised in incorruption... it is sown in weakness; it is raised in power."*

Augustine saw in this that the resurrection body is not *less* physical but *more* real — flesh transfigured by the Spirit, with, at last, *"no further conflict within ourselves."* Read that again slowly. Our deepest suffering is not only external; it is the war inside — between what we want and what we do, between who we are and who we long to be. Paul describes a self where that war is finally, permanently over. Jonathan Edwards named the trajectory: *"Grace is but glory begun, and glory is but grace perfected."* Whatever faint, inconsistent longing toward God you carry now — that is the seed. What is coming is the flower.

And this is not merely personal salvation; it is cosmic reclamation. *"The last enemy that shall be destroyed is death."* Easter is not a past event remembered; it is an ongoing conquest. *"O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?"* — this is not wishful thinking. It is the shout of someone who has seen the empty tomb and understood what it means. And Paul ends not with more theology but with a startling practical turn: *"your labour is not in vain in the Lord."* The resurrection means nothing you do in love is wasted. That kindness offered. That prayer prayed in the dark. None of it is lost, because the God who raised Jesus is the God who raises everything given to Him.

## The Stranger on the Road

Now walk the road to Emmaus — the resurrection story for everyone who has ever walked *away* from hope. Two disciples are leaving Jerusalem, and they speak the most devastating phrase in Scripture: *"we had hoped."* Past tense. The hope is already in the grave. And the Risen Christ does not wait for them to turn around before He comes to them. He meets them in their retreat, in their disappointment, in their wrong direction. He listens to their grief, opens the Scriptures — and they do not recognize Him until He breaks the bread.

Augustine walked that road for decades — toward Carthage, toward ambition — before he could write: *"Late have I loved You, beauty so old and so new... You were within me, and I was outside."* Christ was with him the whole time he was walking away. C. S. Lewis knew the burning heart the disciples felt before they understood it — the inconsolable longing he called *Sehnsucht*: *"If I find in myself a desire which no experience in this world can satisfy, the most probable explanation is that I was made for another world."* The presence is real before the recognition arrives. And Meister Eckhart gave us the most startling line of all: *"God is at home. It is we who have gone out for a walk."*

The Risen Christ is revealed in a meal — bread taken, blessed, broken — and then He vanishes. He disappears so that we stop looking for Him out there and discover Him within. Where has your heart been burning lately — and have you stopped to ask why? Was that a stranger on your road, or the Risen Christ, already opening things to you?

## The Hidden Life

Recognition must become embodiment. *"If ye then be risen with Christ, seek those things which are above... For ye are dead, and your life is hid with Christ in God"* (Colossians 3:1–3). Notice: this is not a command to become something. It is a statement of what you already are, followed by a call to live accordingly. You are already a resurrection person. Now live like one.

*"Set your affection on things above"* — this is Augustine's entire life philosophy in a single verse. We are not primarily thinking creatures who happen to feel; we are loving creatures, constituted by what we love — and our tragedy is that our loves are disordered. *"Two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self... the heavenly by the love of God."* Paul is not calling us to become cold or otherworldly, but to love all things in their right order, with God as the center from which every other love radiates. Every earthly beauty is a signpost pointing beyond itself; we go wrong when we stop at the signpost instead of following where it points.

And here is the most quietly radical thing Paul ever wrote: your real life — the deepest, truest you — is *hidden*. Not your reputation, your achievements, your failures, your circumstances. It is tucked away in God. Safe. Inviolable. Untouchable. That means there is nothing left to prove and nothing left to protect. You are free to be merciful, kind, meek, forgiving — not performing those virtues, but wearing who you already are, the way a raised body wears its glory. Wesley's fearlessness came from exactly here: *"Give me one hundred men who fear nothing but sin and desire nothing but God, and I will shake the world."*

## The Single Question

And now the shortest passage, and the heaviest. It was spoken not on Easter Sunday but before the cross, at the tomb of a dead friend, to a woman paralyzed by grief. Martha believes in resurrection — as a future doctrine, a comfort for the last day. And Jesus steps entirely outside her framework: *"I am the resurrection, and the life... Believest thou this?"* (John 11:25–26).

Not "I will bring about resurrection." Not "I teach it." **I AM it.** Martha was looking to the future for what was standing in front of her face. And the question He asks is not *"Can you explain this?"* or *"What is your theological position?"* — simply: do you believe *this*, the One standing before you? Kierkegaard insisted this question cannot be answered in the third person. It is asked to a single individual, in a specific moment, requiring a specific personal act — the leap, where doctrine must become encounter, where the idea must become trust. And Martha leaps. Not perfectly, not fully understanding: *"Yea, Lord: I believe that thou art the Christ, the Son of God."* That is the whole Christian life in one sentence. Everything else flows from that single yes, spoken imperfectly, in the direction of the One who is asking.

Augustine holds the frame: *"Faith is to believe what you do not see; the reward of this faith is to see what you believe."* And Viktor Frankl, hacking at frozen ground in the grey of a Bavarian dawn, close to death in a concentration camp, heard *"from somewhere a victorious 'Yes' in answer to my question of the existence of an ultimate purpose"* — and at that moment a light was lit in a distant farmhouse. *Et lux in tenebris lucet.* The light shines in the darkness. He had no visible Jesus standing before him; what he had was the last of the human freedoms — to choose his response. And he said yes.

## Go — He Is Risen

So here is the whole arc of Easter, moving from outside to inside, from then to now: the name spoken in the garden; the cosmic victory in which nothing done in love is lost; the stranger who joins us on our wrong roads and is known in the breaking of bread; the life hidden safely in God; and the single question asked of the single soul. **The resurrection is not a past event to be remembered. It is a present reality to be inhabited.**

Spurgeon said, *"I take my text and make a beeline for the cross"* — not because the cross and empty tomb are the finish line, but because they are the starting line. Everything in the Christian life begins there, returns there, and flows from there. And Lewis, at the close of *The Last Battle*, gave us the truest image of what began that Sunday morning: *"The term is over: the holidays have begun. The dream is ended: this is the morning."* All our life in this world is only the cover and the title page; the empty tomb is Chapter One of the Great Story which no one on earth has read, which goes on forever, *in which every chapter is better than the one before.*

So go — as Mary ran from the garden, as Cleopas and his companion ran back to Jerusalem. Back to your life, your people, your ordinary Monday-morning world. Go with a burning heart. Go with a hidden life. Go with the name He has spoken over you ringing in your ears. You are a resurrection person. Your life is hidden in God. Nothing you do in love is wasted. The last enemy is already defeated.

*"Now unto him that is able to do exceeding abundantly above all that we ask or think... unto him be glory in the church by Christ Jesus throughout all ages, world without end. Amen"* (Ephesians 3:20–21).

He is risen. He is risen indeed.
