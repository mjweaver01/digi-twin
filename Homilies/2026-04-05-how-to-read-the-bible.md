# The Veil Lifted: Reading Scripture as an Encounter, Not a Conquest

You open the Bible, and you want to read it well. That desire is itself the beginning of grace — but it comes with a warning attached. It is possible to read this book your whole life and never meet the One who wrote it. Origen of Alexandria, one of the most brilliant minds of the early church, put his finger on the danger seventeen centuries ago: if you only read the Bible on the surface, "a veil lies upon your heart" (2 Corinthians 3:15). But when you turn to the Lord, the veil is lifted, and you behold the glory that was hidden in the text all along.

So the question before us is not merely *how* to read the Bible, but how to read it with the veil lifted. And the great cloud of witnesses who have gone before us — Origen and Augustine, Aquinas and Teresa, Luther, Wesley, Lewis — speak with remarkable unity on this point. Scripture is not a fortress to be stormed by cleverness. It is a voice to be heard, a Person to be met. The Bible is not a book to be conquered; it is an encounter to be entered. Every reading, at every level, is an invitation deeper into the mystery of the God who speaks — and who still speaks today.

## The Word Has Depths

Begin here: the Bible is deeper than it looks. Origen taught that Scripture is like a human being — it has a body, a soul, and a spirit. There is the plain, literal sense of what the text says; there is the moral sense, what it teaches us about how to live; and there is the spiritual sense, the mystical truth hidden within. Even the passages that puzzle or scandalize us on the surface — God "walking in the garden in the cool of the day" — are, Origen insisted, written that way *intentionally*, to push the reader beyond the letter into the spirit. "The sacred books are not the works of men," he wrote, "but were composed and have come down to us as a result of the inspiration of the Holy Spirit."

Thomas Aquinas gave this conviction its enduring architecture. God, unlike human authors, can signify meaning not only through words but through the very things and events those words describe — which is why this book has a depth no other text possesses. The medieval church summed up the four senses of Scripture in a single couplet: *The letter teaches events; allegory, faith; the moral, conduct; the anagogy, our destiny.* Take Israel's exodus from Egypt. Literally, a people left slavery under Moses. Allegorically, Egypt is bondage to sin and the crossing of the sea is baptism. Morally, you are being called to leave your own Egypt — your sins, your attachments. Anagogically, the Promised Land is eternal life and heavenly glory.

But hear Aquinas's crucial caution: all the spiritual senses are grounded in the literal sense. You cannot build an allegory that contradicts what the text plainly says. The literal sense is the foundation; the spiritual senses rise from it like a cathedral from its foundation stones. So do not despise the plain meaning in your rush to the mystical. Read what is written. Then ask of every passage: What happened? What does this reveal about Christ? What must I do? What does this point to in eternity?

And ask one question more — the question Philo of Alexandria taught long before the church was born: *Who am I in this story?* For Philo, the characters of Scripture are not only historical figures but living symbols of the soul's journey. Abraham is the soul leaving the land of mere opinion and setting out toward wisdom. The Exodus is the soul's liberation from the passions that enslave it. Moses ascending Sinai is the mind rising in contemplation toward God. Read the Bible as a mirror of your own soul. What Egypt are you leaving? What Sinai are you climbing? And take comfort in Philo's most tender insight: when the sun of your own understanding sets — when you have begun to despair of your own strength — that is precisely when the divine Word comes to meet you, aid arriving invisibly from without.

## Read with Rules, and Read with Love

Depth without discipline breeds fantasy, and so Augustine gives us the rules. When you meet an obscure passage, do not panic and do not invent: consult the rule of faith gathered from the *plainer* passages of Scripture. The Bible interprets itself — let the clear illuminate the unclear. Learn to interpret, Augustine says, the way a child learns to read: so that you will not always need someone else to lay open the secret to you, but may arrive at the hidden sense yourself.

Yet Augustine's supreme rule of interpretation is not a technique at all. It is this: any reading of Scripture that does not build up love of God and neighbor is not yet the true meaning of the text. If your reading of the Bible does not lead you to love more deeply, keep digging. You can be exegetically precise and spiritually bankrupt. Love is the compass; every faithful interpretation points toward it, because the God who wrote the book is love, and "You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our hearts are restless until they rest in You."

## Look for Christ on Every Page

Martin Luther hands us the interpretive key that unlocks the whole library: all of Scripture points to Jesus Christ. The types, the shadows, the laws, the prophecies of the Old Testament find their meaning in him. Reading the Bible without Christ is like reading a book without understanding its central character. That the serpent lifted up by Moses signifies Christ — this, Luther said, we learn not from ourselves but from the Holy Spirit himself.

And Luther insists the book is *yours*. No pope, tradition, or council stands above it; you do not need a priest to mediate it for you. So be, in his wonderful phrase, a "Text-man" — someone well grounded in the actual words: "Draw water out of the true fountain — diligently read the Bible... he that is well grounded in the text, the same hath whereupon he surely may keep footing." For Luther this was never academic. When temptation and trouble came, he reached immediately for a text of Scripture: "I quickly lay hold and fasten on some text... that He died for me, from whence I have and receive comfort." Let the Word be your anchor in the storm, not merely your subject in the study.

## Read Slowly, Read Praying

Here Teresa of Ávila takes us by the hand, for she approaches Scripture not as an intellectual project but as an encounter with a Person — the living God who dwells in the innermost room of the soul. This is the heart of *Lectio Divina*, the church's ancient art of sacred reading, and its four movements are simple enough for a child. *Lectio*: read a short passage slowly, even aloud — not to cover ground, but to listen. *Meditatio*: chew on the word or phrase that stirs you; repeat it; let it sink in. *Oratio*: speak to God from what the text has stirred, until reading becomes conversation. *Contemplatio*: then stop speaking. Rest silently in his presence. Just be with him.

"Mental prayer," Teresa wrote, "is nothing else than an intimate friendship, a frequent heart-to-heart conversation with Him by whom we know ourselves to be loved." She told of a simple nun who could manage only vocal prayers — yet while praying the *Our Father*, God raised her to the heights of contemplation. Mark the lesson well: the depth of your reading is determined not by your intellect but by the sincerity of your attention and love. The great obstacle is pride — thinking you can master the text by cleverness. The great aid is humility — approaching the Word as a beggar approaches a King, knowing you need what only he can give. "Let nothing disturb you, let nothing frighten you. All things pass away. God never changes."

## Let the Story Work on You

C.S. Lewis, literary scholar and late convert, reminds us of something the pious too easily forget: the Bible is not one book but a library — poetry and law, history and prophecy, wisdom, letters, apocalypse. Reading Genesis the way you read Romans is like reading a Shakespeare play the way you read a legal brief. Read the Psalms as poetry to be felt and prayed, not propositions to be analyzed. Read the Epistles as letters. Read the Gospels as eyewitness testimony. The parables are not mere illustrations; they are windows into a reality that must be *entered*.

Lewis had long dismissed the Gospels as just another myth — until, in conversation with Tolkien, he saw the astonishing thing: the Gospel is the myth that became fact. All the longing that the great myths point toward — sacrifice, death, resurrection, the return of the king — actually happened, in history, in Jesus of Nazareth. So read imaginatively; let the images and archetypes work on your heart, not just your reason. And read the old books alongside the new — the Fathers, the medieval commentators — lest you see Scripture only through the blind spots of your own age. "I believe in Christianity as I believe that the sun has risen: not only because I see it, but because by it I see everything else."

## Has It Changed You?

Finally, John Wesley stands at the door as you close the book, asking the one question that judges all the others: *Is the Bible actually changing you?* Wesley honored tradition, reason, and experience, but held Scripture supreme over them all — and he was alarmed by people who could recite doctrine perfectly while their lives showed no change, no love, no holiness; who had, as he put it, lost the eyes of the soul. What Scripture is *for* is not theological expertise but the heart strangely warmed — the assurance Wesley himself received at Aldersgate: "I felt I did trust in Christ, Christ alone for salvation; and an assurance was given me that He had taken away my sins."

So after every reading, ask Wesley's question: Has this changed anything in me? Is my love for God greater? Is my love for my neighbor greater? Am I more holy than I was? If not, keep reading — but keep praying for the Spirit to make the Word alive in you. And do not read alone forever. Bring what you find to brothers and sisters who can see your blind spots and stir up your growth, for the Word was given to a people, not only to persons.

## Go, and Read

Here, then, is the whole counsel gathered into a way of life. Before you read, pray — invite the Holy Spirit to lift the veil; be still; choose a small, manageable portion. As you read, go slowly; ask what the text plainly says, what it reveals of Christ, what it calls you to do, what it promises of eternity; ask who you are in the story; let the images work on your imagination; look for Christ everywhere. After you read, let a word become a prayer, and then be silent. Then ask what must change in you — and share what you have found. Over time, read the whole Bible, not just your favorite parts; read a little daily rather than rarely in large doses; read alongside the great commentators; and return to the same passages again and again, for the same text will speak differently as you grow. Scripture is not information but formation.

"For the word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing to the division of soul and of spirit" (Hebrews 4:12). That is not a description of a book you can conquer. It is a description of a sword that reads *you*. Come to it, then, not as a scholar storming a fortress but as a beggar before a King, a child before a Father, a restless heart before the only One in whom it can rest. Open the book. Turn to the Lord. And the veil will be lifted.
