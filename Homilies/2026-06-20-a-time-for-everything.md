# Eternity in a Heart of Seasons

There is a passage of Scripture so familiar that we risk no longer hearing it. *"To everything there is a season, and a time for every purpose under heaven: a time to be born and a time to die; a time to plant and a time to uproot; a time to kill and a time to heal; a time to tear down and a time to build; a time to weep and a time to laugh; a time to mourn and a time to dance."* So begins Ecclesiastes 3, and so it continues — a time to embrace and a time to refrain from embracing, a time to keep silence and a time to speak, a time of war and a time of peace.

Qohelet, the Preacher, gives us twenty-eight moments arranged in fourteen couplets — a perfect poem of cosmic rhythm, opposites paired like the swing of a pendulum. This is no accident of style. It is a claim about the shape of your life: that your existence is not a straight line of progress but a turning wheel of appointed seasons, and that wisdom begins when you learn to read the hour you are actually in.

## The Discipline of Discernment

The ancients knew this poem cannot be read on one level alone, and neither should we. On the plainest level it is simply true: there really are seasons in a human life. Birth and death, war and peace, planting and uprooting — these are historically real, and we must not spiritualize them away. Someone hearing these words today is in a season of mourning that no metaphor will soften. The text honors that.

But beneath the literal lies the moral. Each verse is a call to *discernment*. The virtuous person knows *which* time it is. To laugh when you should mourn, or to speak when you should be silent, is not merely awkward — it is a failure of wisdom. This passage is a training ground for the soul.

And Origen of Alexandria, the great interpreter, pressed deeper still. In his vision of *apokatastasis* — the universal restoration of all things — these seasons trace the very rhythm of the soul's journey toward God. Even our descents — our times of weeping, tearing down, casting away — are not punishment but **education**. "Perhaps they had not yet fulfilled the appropriate time," he wrote of souls still wandering, "to be called to a more enduring repentance." Time itself is a divine teacher, and the seasons of suffering are precisely the seasons in which the soul is ripened for return to God. Hear that clearly: God does not waste your winters.

## Stop Fighting the Season You Are In

Even the pagan world glimpsed this truth. Marcus Aurelius, writing alone in his imperial tent, meditated constantly on time's flow: *"Some things hasten to be, and others to be no more... we must not think otherwise of our lives, than as a mere exhalation of blood, or an ordinary respiration of air."* And more strikingly: *"The time of a man's life is as a point; the substance of it ever flowing... Our life is a warfare, and a mere pilgrimage."*

From this the emperor drew the very lesson of Ecclesiastes 3: **stop fighting the season you are in.** There is a time to weep — weep fully. There is a time for war — engage with duty. The wise soul does not resist the current of nature but flows with it, keeping the inner citadel undisturbed. *"Perpetual fluxes and alterations renew the world,"* Marcus wrote, *"as the perpetual course of time doth make the age of the world to appear always fresh and new."* This is *amor fati* — love of fate: not passive resignation, but an active embrace of what *is*, because what is, is the universe unfolding according to the Logos. Marcus and Qohelet agree across the centuries: to resist your season is folly; to live fully within it is wisdom.

How much of our misery comes from being out of season — grieving what we should release, grasping what we should cast away, speaking when the hour asks for silence? The Preacher does not tell us to escape the seasons. He tells us to inhabit them.

## The Seasons Within

The mystics of Israel heard something more in these verses. In the Zohar's vision, time is not merely chronological — it is *ontological*, a window into the very structure of divine reality. Each moment corresponds to a configuration of the divine emanations, the Sefirot: "a time to embrace and a time to refrain" reflects the interplay of Chesed's expansive mercy and Gevurah's contracting judgment; "a time for war and a time for peace" reflects the cosmic tension between the Other Side and the realm of holiness. The fourteen couplets are not merely human observations — they are a **map of divine rhythm**.

And the Zohar reveals something breathtaking about time itself: eternity "refers at once to our past, present, and future, for time exists only with regard to those who are still confined to their egoistic desires." Qohelet's seasons, then, point to **spiritual states**, not merely calendar moments. The *time to die* may happen while you are biologically living — it may be the death of the ego. The *time to be born* may be a spiritual rebirth in the middle years of an ordinary life. Read this way, the poem becomes a ladder of inner transformation. So ask yourself honestly: what in you is being uprooted right now? What is being planted? What must be torn before it can be sewn?

Three voices — the theologian, the emperor, the mystic — and one converging teaching: every season, even the season of suffering, is divinely appointed for the soul's education; the wise flow with the season rather than resisting it; and time itself is a spiritual reality drawing all things toward unity.

## The Ache That Proves Our Origin

But we must not miss this: Qohelet is not celebrating the seasons — he is **unsettled** by them. Immediately after his beautiful poem, he asks in verse 9: *"What profit has the worker from all his toil?"* And then verse 11 delivers the thunderbolt:

> *"He has made everything beautiful in its time. He has also set eternity in the human heart; yet no one can fathom what God has done from beginning to end."*

This is the great tension at the heart of the passage — and at the heart of you. **God has placed eternity inside us**, yet we are creatures of time, unable to see the whole picture. We feel the ache of every season *precisely because* we were made for something beyond all seasons. The grief of loss, the joy of birth, the confusion of each changing chapter — these are the fingerprints of a soul that was made for the Eternal, living in the temporal.

That ache is not a malfunction. It is a homing signal. If nothing in this turning world fully satisfies you, it is because you were not made to be satisfied by turning things. Qohelet calls it all *hevel* — breath, vapor — not because life is worthless, but because it will not hold still long enough to be grasped. The seasons are beautiful, but they are not your home. They are the road.

## Trust the Appointed Time

So here is the word for you today. Whatever season you find yourself in — planting or uprooting, mourning or dancing, silence or speech — do three things.

First, *name it honestly*. Discernment begins with telling the truth about which time it is, not the time you wish it were.

Second, *inhabit it fully*. Do not weep halfway or build halfheartedly. The season will not be rushed, and it will not be repeated. Flow with it; do not fight it.

Third, *hold it loosely*. Every season under heaven ends — that is both its nature and its mercy. But the eternity set in your heart does not end. Live in time as one who belongs to something beyond it.

*"He has made everything beautiful in its time."* Whatever season you find yourself in, trust that it is *beautiful* in its appointed time, even when you cannot yet see how. The One who appointed the seasons has not lost track of yours. Go, then, and live this hour as the hour it truly is — and let the ache of eternity keep pulling you home.
