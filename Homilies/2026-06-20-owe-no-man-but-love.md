# The Debt That Is Never Paid

There is a kind of freedom in a settled account. The loan repaid, the invoice cleared, the last installment made — done. Finished. Nothing hangs over you. Paul knows this feeling, and in Romans 13 he commands us to pursue it: *"Render therefore to all their dues: tribute to whom tribute is due; custom to whom custom; fear to whom fear; honour to whom honour"* (Romans 13:7). Pay everyone what you owe them — taxes, customs, respect, honor. Settle your accounts. Be a person of integrity.

And then, in the very next breath, he names the one exception:

> *"Owe no man any thing, but to love one another: for he that loveth another hath fulfilled the law."* (Romans 13:8)

There is one debt you must never consider paid — the debt of love.

## A Debt You Can Never Fully Discharge

Here is the brilliant paradox at the heart of the verse. Every other debt, once paid, is *finished*. You pay your taxes — done. You repay a loan — done. But the debt of love is perpetually renewable. The moment you show love to someone, you haven't discharged the debt — you've simply *expressed* it. It renews itself each day, with each person, in each moment.

You will never stand before your neighbor and say, "I have loved you enough; we are square now." Yesterday's kindness does not cover today's obligation. The account never closes. Paul is calling us to live in a constant state of loving indebtedness to one another — not as a burden that crushes, but as the very shape of the Christian life.

## Love Fulfills the Law — It Does Not Abolish It

But how can one debt stand in for all the commandments? This is Paul's masterstroke. He does not say *ignore* the commandments. He says love comprehends them all:

> *"For this, Thou shalt not commit adultery, Thou shalt not kill, Thou shalt not steal, Thou shalt not covet; and if there be any other commandment, it is briefly comprehended in this saying, namely, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself."* (Romans 13:9)

Consider each in turn. *Don't commit adultery* — love your spouse, honor their dignity. *Don't murder* — love your neighbor, honor their life. *Don't steal* — love others enough to respect what is theirs. *Don't covet* — love others enough to rejoice in their blessings.

The logic is elegant: a person truly animated by love cannot help but keep the law, because every commandment is simply love expressing itself in a specific situation. You don't need a rule against murder if you genuinely love your neighbor. The law is the *map*; love is the *destination*. And so Paul can conclude: *"Love worketh no ill to his neighbour: therefore love is the fulfilling of the law"* (Romans 13:10).

## What Kind of Love Can Be Commanded?

Perhaps you object: love cannot be commanded. Feelings come and go; no decree can summon affection. And you would be right — if Paul meant a feeling.

C.S. Lewis, in *The Four Loves*, distinguishes four kinds of love: affection (*storge*), the warmth of familiarity; friendship (*philia*), the love of shared truth; romantic love (*eros*), passionate longing; and charity (*agape*), unconditional, self-giving love. The love Paul commands in Romans 13 is *agape*. This is not a feeling you wait to have. It is not romantic attraction or even natural affection. It is a willed commitment to the good of another person, regardless of whether they deserve it, regardless of how you feel.

Lewis put the posture memorably: *"Humility is not thinking less of yourself, but thinking of yourself less."* Agape is exactly this — not a crushing of the self, but a *reorientation* of the self outward. It is the love that loves the unlovable, gives without requiring return, and works no ill even when ill is deserved.

This is why Paul can *command* it. You cannot command a feeling. But you can command a posture of the will — a decision, renewed each morning, to seek the good of the other. And if you wonder what that decision looks like when it walks around in a life, Paul has already told us: *"Love is patient and kind; love does not envy or boast; it is not arrogant or rude... Love bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things"* (1 Corinthians 13).

## Why We Owe It: The Debt Behind the Debt

Yet there is a deeper dimension still. Why should I stand in perpetual debt to my neighbor — even the neighbor who has done nothing for me, even the one who has wronged me?

Because the debt of love is not, in the end, a debt to the neighbor at all. Paul writes elsewhere: *"The love of God is shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost which is given unto us"* (Romans 5:5). We love because we have been loved first. The debt of love we owe one another is ultimately rooted in the love God has poured into us. To love our neighbor is not merely a moral obligation — it is a *participation in the divine nature itself*, divine life expressed through human relationship.

So the verse works on three levels at once. Ethically: pay all your worldly debts, but let love remain an ever-renewing obligation. Legally: love is the spirit behind every commandment, so that to love is to fulfill the whole law. Theologically: love flows from God's love within us, and every act of neighbor-love is God's own life moving through you.

Paul's genius is in making love not a sentimental feeling but the most serious moral category in existence — a debt that honors every person as someone worthy of the infinite love that God himself bears toward them.

## Go as a Debtor

So settle every account you have in this world — except one. Pay the tax, return the loan, give honor where honor is due, and let no creditor have a claim on you. But when you come to the ledger marked *love*, do not reach for the pen that writes "paid in full." That line stays open. Every act of love does not discharge the debt but declares it, renewing the obligation with each person, each day, each moment.

You cannot be commanded to feel. But you can be commanded to will the good of another — and when you do, you have kept the whole law without needing to count its commandments.

Go, then, as a debtor: owing everyone nothing but love, because Someone first loved you beyond all repayment.
