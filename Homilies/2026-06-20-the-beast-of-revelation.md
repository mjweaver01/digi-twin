# Every Babylon Falls: The Beast, the Harlot, and the Two Cities

There is a page near the end of your Bible where a woman rides a monster. She is dressed in purple and scarlet, adorned with gold and jewels, seated on many waters, drunk — the text says — with the blood of the saints. On her forehead is written a name: *"Babylon the Great, the Mother of Harlots and of Earth's Abominations."* Beneath her, the Beast: seven heads, ten horns, crowns upon crowns, a body stitched together from leopard and bear and lion, given its power by the Dragon himself, making war on the saints and demanding what belongs to God alone — worship. And behind them both, a second beast, the False Prophet, pressing a mark onto every hand and forehead, so that no one may buy or sell without it.

Most of us close the book at that page. It frightens us, or it embarrasses us, or it has been handled so badly by so many prediction-mongers that we no longer trust ourselves to touch it. But hear this: Revelation was not written to give you nightmares, and it was not written as a puzzle-map of future geopolitics. It was written as a revelation — an unveiling — of what is true in every age, every empire, and every soul. Including yours.

## The Cipher and the City

Start where the first readers started. To a persecuted church in the first century, the Beast and Babylon were a coded portrait of Imperial Rome. "Babylon" was already a well-worn Jewish and Christian code name for Rome, for both empires had done the unthinkable — both had destroyed Jerusalem's Temple, Babylon in 586 BC and Rome in 70 AD. Even the dreaded number 666 yields its secret to the ancient practice of gematria, by which letters carry numeric values: spell "Neron Caesar" in Hebrew and the letters sum to 666 — Nero, the emperor who burned Christians as torches. The seven heads? The seven hills of Rome, a succession of emperors demanding divine honors.

So the vision names a real tyrant. But if that were all it named, Revelation would have expired with Nero. It did not expire, because the Spirit was naming something older than Rome and newer than tomorrow's headlines.

## The Beast Within

Origen of Alexandria taught that Scripture always speaks on more than one level — the literal, the moral, and the spiritual — and that the deepest reading never stops at history. "When God is said to walk in the paradise in the cool of the day," he wrote, "I do not think anyone will doubt that these are figurative expressions which indicate certain mysteries through a semblance of history." So too with Revelation. Its beasts, numbers, and cities are vessels of deeper truths, and the conflict it unveils is not merely Rome versus the Christians — it is a cosmic drama of souls.

Hear what that means for you. The Beast is not only out there in the empire; it is the force of spiritual corruption that operates within history, within institutions, and within the human heart. Babylon is the condition of a soul — or a civilization — captive to material passions, idolatry, and the love of self over God. And the mark of the Beast is not merely an economic stamp on the skin. It is a soul stamped with the image of worldliness rather than the image of God. To receive the mark is to be formed by the Beast's values: power, domination, the worship of the created over the Creator. You do not need a barcode on your forehead to bear it. You need only let the empire tell you what to love.

## Two Cities, Two Loves

When Rome — eternal Rome, invincible Rome — was sacked by the Visigoths in 410, the world reeled, and Augustine answered with the *City of God*. History, he said, is the story of two cities locked in perpetual tension: "Two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self."

Babylon is the City of Man — built on pride, on self-love, on the *libido dominandi*, the lust for domination. It is not simply ancient Babylon, and not simply Rome. It is every human empire, every institution, every heart that elevates itself above God, every civilization that mistakes temporal power for ultimate meaning. And the Beast is the spirit of that city made manifest: political power that demands total allegiance, displacing the worship of God with the worship of the state, the culture, the emperor of the hour.

This is why the fall of Babylon is not a single event on some prophecy chart. It is the inevitable, eternal fate of every project built on self-love — it collapses under its own weight. "Fallen, fallen is Babylon the Great!" is not a prediction so much as a law of the moral universe.

And the City of God? She is the Church pilgriming through history — never fully at home in any earthly empire, sojourning "as a stranger in the midst of the ungodly," always oriented toward her eternal rest. Do not miss the hard word in this: the two cities are commingled in history — in nations, in institutions, even in the Church. No flag, no party, no denomination is simply the City of God. The line between the cities runs through every human heart.

## Weighed in the Balances

The Reformers pressed the warning to its most uncomfortable point. Luther, Calvin, Knox, and after them Jonathan Edwards, dared to say that even the Church can become Babylon — that any institution, however holy its vestments, which places itself between the soul and God, demanding worship and obedience due only to Christ, has become Babylonian in spirit. The Beast, for Edwards, was Antichrist — not necessarily one future individual, but a principle and spirit of opposition to Christ operating through institutional religion and political power alike. In his *History of the Work of Redemption* he read all of history as Christ's Kingdom advancing against the powers of darkness, and over every proud power he heard the same sentence Daniel once read on a palace wall:

*"The doom went forth, and the record against her was written as against Great Babylon of old — 'MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN.' God hath numbered thy kingdom and finished it. Thou art weighed in the balances and found wanting."*

No earthly power that sets itself against God's purposes will endure. The fall of Babylon is the fall of every tyranny — political, religious, spiritual. Every one.

## Whose Mark Do You Bear?

So what does this ancient vision demand of us? Three things, and a promise.

First: **empires end.** Every Babylon — however glorious, however gold-plated, however inevitable it looks from inside — falls. *"In one hour your judgment has come"* (Rev. 18:10). Whatever gleams with purple and scarlet today is already weighed in the balances. Do not mortgage your soul to it.

Second: **the mark of the Beast is a question of allegiance.** The question Revelation puts to you is not "When will the end come?" but "Whose mark do you bear?" Who or what shapes your identity, your buying and selling, your vision of the good life? If the answer is power, wealth, nation, or self — then you already know which city has stamped you.

Third: **discern and choose.** Because the two cities are mingled together in this age — in your country, your church, your own heart — the Christian life is a daily act of discernment: to detect the *libido dominandi* wherever it hides, even in you, and to choose again the city formed by the love of God.

And now the promise, for the apocalypse is not finally a horror story. Even Origen, staring into its darkest imagery, saw shining beyond it the *apokatastasis* — the final restoration of all things to God. The drama does not end with the smoke of Babylon's burning. It ends with a new heaven and a new earth, with the New Jerusalem descending like a bride — the anti-Babylon, the city built not on domination but on love, where God Himself dwells with His people.

The harlot rides the Beast for a season. The saints walk as strangers for a season. But the last word of the Bible is not ruin; it is a wedding. Every Babylon falls. The Lamb wins. See to it, beloved, that when the balances are read, you are found a citizen of the city that cannot fall.
